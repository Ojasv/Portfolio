01 · The challenge

Many teams, many tools, one dataset.

Bosch curates huge sets of camera images to train the models behind its driver assistance (ADAS) features. Curation was spread across several separate applications, each run by a different team: one to bring data in, one to label it, one to build filter models, another to evaluate them. Every hand-over between tools was a place where time, context and data quality could leak away.

We were not starting from scratch. The job was to design an internal platform that streamlined curation at every touchpoint of the data pipeline, while also improving the quality of what the models put out.

  • Efficiency

    Streamline the workflow

    Cut repeated steps and hand-overs so the whole process moves faster.

  • Transparency

    Make information flow visible

    Show where each dataset is, who has worked on it and what state it is in.

  • Motivation

    Help people finish tasks sooner

    Give clear progress and a clear finish line, so tasks get closed, not parked.

  • Every touchpoint

    Fix curation step by step

    Find the key challenges at each stage of curation and solve them where they happen.

02 · One pipeline, two apps

Operationally very similar. Functionally very different.

In the same data pipeline, the work was split into two applications. When I ran a workshop with the whole data-pipeline cohort, one idea kept coming back: do not confuse search ranking with label optimisation. In both, people look at crops of images and accept or reject them. But what that decision trains is completely different.

  • ATLAS · Image optimisation

    Starts from labels. Asks: is this label right?

    Data arrives grouped by label and is plotted as clusters. Accepting or rejecting a crop corrects labels, exposes anomalies and tunes the filter models. There is no search here, on purpose.

  • CLIFF · Image search

    Starts from a query. Asks: is this what I searched for?

    Later in the pipeline, an upvote or downvote on a search result trains the search model to rank the right images higher, before tickets go to preselection.

03 · How ATLAS works

ATLAS sits early in the data pipeline, where raw recordings become labelled crops and filter models are built. It is where people first see their data as a whole.

  1. 1

    Bring in a recording run

    Take one run recorded in Japan, by a vehicle with a 360° camera and LIDAR.

    For ATLAS we analysed the camera frames from different angles, the crops taken from them, and the LIDAR depth output together with each image.

  2. 2

    Label everything automatically

    Frames from all the sources are labelled by an automatic labelling service.

    Crops are generated for each label, so one frame can give a traffic light, a speed sign and a truck, each as its own crop.

  3. 3

    Start from a label

    Data is populated by label. A traffic sign dataset, for example, holds Stop, Right turn, Slow down, Speed limit 30 and more.

    There is no search box, on purpose. Search comes later in the pipeline, in CLIFF.

  4. 4

    Plot the distribution

    Filter the traffic light label, and ATLAS plots every traffic light detected in every frame.

    The same crops can be laid out as t-SNE, k-means t-SNE, a grid, RasterFairy or UMAP. Red lights fall into one cluster, green and amber into others.

  5. 5

    Check the labels, find the anomalies

    The plot shows at a glance whether crops sit where their label says they should.

    It is not only a check on label correctness. Crops that land in the wrong cluster reveal anomalies in how the data was filtered.

  6. 6

    Improve the filter models and re-rank

    Filter models are developed and optimised on this data.

    Crops in the filtered subset are accepted or rejected, then re-ranked, which optimises the confidence scores of the crops for the next model.

04 · Why labels, not search

A narrow search optimises a narrow slice.

Why start from labels and not from a search? If someone searched for one specific sign and then visualised the results, they might get only a few crops, and the optimisation would have little to learn from.

Working from a broader label changes that. Optimising street lights as a whole, rather than only red lights, improves the ranks of the crops far more holistically, because the model sees the whole population and the differences inside it.

06 · Who we designed for

Four roles, one pipeline, and each of them sees only their part of it.

Once we understood the product, we turned to the people using it and how they saw the task flow. We found four user segments: labelling experts, filter model developers, feature model developers and data analysts.

  • Jack

    Data analyst

    Curates datasets and runs the filter models with the highest confidence scores to validate the labels. Checks tags by eye in the visualisation to pick the right filter model for each dataset.

    Needs

    • A clear, high-level view of the whole pipeline, from raw data to final output
    • Reliable metrics to validate the accuracy and quality of curated data

    Frustrations

    • No visibility into where the bottlenecks are
    • Checking and validating tags by hand is slow and tedious

  • Suyansh

    Filter model developer

    Develops the filter models that pull images out of large datasets. Models are compared, and the ones with the highest confidence scores are deployed for filtering sessions.

    Needs

    • A way to see a filter model's output and judge its performance quickly
    • Feedback on how models perform later in the pipeline

    Frustrations

    • Hard to integrate new models into the existing application
    • Without good visualisation, it is hard to see why a model degrades on some data

  • Dennis

    Labelling expert

    Handles labelling for huge datasets. Manages the labelling sets, checks the labels marked for review, and adds or removes crops based on how relevant they are to each label.

  • Rakshit

    Feature model developer

    Refines the dataset for one feature at a time. He looks at the feature, finds the right cluster of images and tags them to improve it.

    Needs

    • Query and extract exact subsets of data by tag and feature
    • Build and manage custom data streams for different ADAS applications

    Frustrations

    • Limited search and filtering make the right data hard to find
    • No lineage: he cannot see which models or experts have touched the data

07 · Workshops

Putting everyone in the same room, on the same map.

Each user type joined a series of workshops to describe their own workflow and the challenges at each step of curation and optimisation. Together we mapped one journey across the whole pipeline, and for every stage we noted what people do, what they need, what frustrates them, the tools they touch, how they feel and where the opportunities are.

  • Data selection
  • Ingestion and quality check
  • Sampling and filtering
  • Curation for training
  • Training the model
  • Evaluating the model
  • Model deployment

The stages of the journey map, with a feedback loop from evaluation back to the model.

Then we brought all the users together in common sessions, where each person walked through the others' workflows as role-play journeys against the clock. Seeing each other's work helped us remove repeated steps, and built empathy between people at different ends of the pipeline, so the whole process got quicker and worked towards one goal.

08 · Design

Structure first, then the Bosch design language.

Wireframes gave stakeholders the base structure of each screen so they could give feedback early, before any visual design. Once the wireframes were agreed, we adapted them to the theming and components of the Bosch design system, so the new tools felt like part of the same family as everything else the teams used.
