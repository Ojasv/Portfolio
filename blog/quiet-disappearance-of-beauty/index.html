The quiet disappearance of beauty

A designer’s reflection on our function-first world.

I’ve been thinking a lot lately about the world we’re building around ourselves, both physically and digitally. As a product designer turned UX designer, I spend most of my days immersed in decisions about function, efficiency, usability, growth, and performance. But somewhere along the way, I’ve started to notice something uncomfortable, almost unsettling:

We are slowly losing beauty.

Not beauty in the abstract, poetic sense, but beauty in the everyday, right-in-front-of-you sense.
Beauty in the objects we interact with.
Beauty in the streets we walk on.
Beauty in the interfaces we use all day.
Beauty in the built world that shapes our moods, our emotions, and our sense of belonging.

It’s becoming harder to find.

And the more I look around, the clearer it becomes: function has overtaken form, and efficiency has eclipsed delight. We are designing things that work, but we are no longer designing things that stay with you.

The lamppost that says everything

Let’s take something incredibly mundane: a lamppost.

Walk down a modern street and you’ll see it: plain, cylindrical, cold, purely functional. A minimal metal pole with an LED head slapped on top. It does its job, yes. It illuminates. It complies with standards. It survives harsh weather. It’s cost-effective.

But stand next to a Victorian-era lamppost (if you can still find one) and compare the experience.

The earlier lamppost:

  • was more than just a pole
  • had ornamentation
  • had character
  • had a certain charm
  • added to the street rather than merely existing on it

Functionally, it might not have been the most efficient light source in human history. But it had a sense of being designed rather than produced.

That difference matters.
Because one of them lights the street.
And the other lights the imagination.

When beauty was a civic value

There’s a romantic irony in history that I often come back to.

We assume that pre-industrial societies (many of them hierarchical, monarchic, or even chaotic) were less considerate of the common person. Yet these civilizations consistently gave their people remarkably beautiful environments.

Think of:

  • intricately carved door frames
  • hand-finished houses
  • richly ornamented public fountains
  • local craft guilds that shaped everyday tools
  • streets designed not only for movement but for memory

Even if you weren’t wealthy, your surroundings carried intention. There was a belief that beauty improves life for everyone, and that awe was not a luxury reserved for the elite.

Fast-forward to today.
A world that is (on paper) far more democratic and equal.
And yet, visually, we’ve become strangely hierarchical again.

Democracy in principle, hierarchy in aesthetic

Walk through any modern city. Pay attention to the buildings.

Common residential blocks?

  • perfect rectangles
  • flat façades
  • standard 7-foot doors
  • minimal or no ornamentation
  • built purely around cost calculations and efficiency metrics

Now look at the homes of the wealthy, or at culturally significant institutional buildings:

  • tall, oversized doors
  • sweeping arches
  • expansive surfaces that don’t “need” to be that way
  • expressive materials
  • spaces designed to evoke emotion

It’s the same society, the same city, the same era, but with completely different design philosophies.

The everyday person lives inside efficiency.
The powerful live inside beauty.

It’s not that we lack resources. It’s that we’ve stopped seeing beauty as something everyone deserves. We have started to lose our sense of purpose.

The problem with our function-first design culture

Having spent years inside the machinery of design teams, I can tell you exactly where this shift comes from.

Today’s design conversations revolve around:

  • time-to-market
  • scalability
  • performance
  • standardization
  • conversion
  • maintainability
  • margins

We obsess over:

  • reducing clicks
  • optimizing flows
  • minimizing cost
  • maximizing output

In this environment, beauty becomes optional.
Or expensive.
Or “nice to have.”
Or “maybe after launch.”

But design is not just the capacity to function.
It’s the capacity to resonate.

Beauty is not waste. It’s emotional infrastructure.

We forget that people don’t love things merely because they work. They love things because they make them feel something.

What happens when everything is efficient, but nothing is beautiful?

There’s a quiet emotional cost to living in a relentlessly functional world.

When every object is optimized, but none of them is meaningful…
When every building is efficient, but none of them inspires…
When every digital interface is frictionless, but none of them is joyful…

…we slowly flatten the human experience.

Beauty reminds us that the world is worth caring about.
Function alone reminds us that the world is worth managing.

Look around carefully: more and more of our surroundings are falling into the second category.

A future that might swing back to form

Now here’s the interesting twist.
I don’t believe this is permanent.

When a society becomes overwhelmingly functional, when everything around us is optimized, automated, and quantified, something inside people begins to crave the opposite.

I see it already in younger designers:

  • a renewed interest in craftsmanship
  • a desire for tactile, warm materials
  • a push for emotional depth in interfaces
  • a fascination with form beyond grids and templates

Even popular media portrays future worlds not as plain and industrial, but as either:

  • glowing, expressive, and identity-rich
  • or stark and dystopian in a way that makes you miss beauty

Either way, the message is clear:
the future is not indifferent to form.

If anything, future generations may seek beauty more aggressively, because they’ll live in a world overflowing with information, automation, and functionality. In that saturation, beauty becomes rebellion.

Global events do shape aesthetics

As designers, we often talk about trends, movements, waves, and eras, but we sometimes forget that the biggest force shaping aesthetics isn’t design theory at all.
It’s history.
It’s crisis.
It’s conflict.
It’s the collective shocks that force entire civilizations to rethink how they live.

History shows us that these global disruptions always leave aesthetic fingerprints.

World Wars: when function became survival

Take World War I and World War II, arguably the most transformative events for design in the last 150 years.

Before the wars, European design was steeped in ornamentation: Art Nouveau’s flowing lines, classical façades, intricate furniture, handcrafted everyday objects. Beauty was a cultural expectation.

Then came wartime shortages:

  • metals were rationed
  • factories were repurposed
  • raw materials were scarce
  • mass production needed to be fast and cheap
  • the focus shifted to durability, efficiency, and scale

This wasn’t just an industrial shift. It was a psychological one.

Suddenly, ornament felt irresponsible. Aesthetics felt indulgent.
Function became necessity, survival, patriotism.

And when the wars ended, the mindset lingered.

The Bauhaus, the Ulm School, and later mid-century modernism weren’t random movements. They were cultural recalibrations:

  • no excess
  • no flourishes
  • no unnecessary detail
  • clarity, honesty, rationality
  • form strictly following function

Design became democratic.
But it also became stripped down.

Many of the plain, industrial objects we see today carry the silent legacy of wartime pragmatism.

And yet, here’s the beautiful twist.

Some cultures reclaimed poetic design

Despite the global push toward efficiency, certain cultures emerged from war with a renewed hunger for beauty, almost as a healing response. And their designs became iconic.

Italy: poetic, romantic, human

Italy suffered greatly during the wars, yet something remarkable happened in the aftermath: the explosion of Bel Design, a uniquely Italian celebration of beauty, sensuality, and emotional expression.

Think of the Bialetti Moka Pot.
It’s not just a coffee maker. It’s a piece of sculpture, a tiny architectural object sitting on the stove.

Its form is:

  • geometric yet warm
  • precise yet expressive
  • functional yet proudly aesthetic

It wasn’t enough to make coffee efficiently.
It needed to feel like a ritual.
It needed to bring pleasure to the kitchen.
It needed to assert that even after devastation, life should be beautiful.

Italian design became a cultural recovery, a way to reclaim humanity after trauma.

Germany: rational, precise, engineered

Germany’s post-war story was different.
Rebuilding society required trust in order, structure, and reliability, and German design became a reflection of that mindset.

Think about Porsche, a brand that embodies:

  • engineering clarity
  • functional purity
  • timeless proportions
  • quiet confidence
  • restrained elegance

It’s beauty born from discipline, not ornament. Where Italy embraced emotional curves, Germany embraced exacting lines. Yet both are beautiful, because both reflect authentic cultural stories.

Italian beauty is expression.
German beauty is precision.

Both emerged from the scars of conflict, shaped by what their people needed emotionally during reconstruction.

Design is a mirror of collective emotion

When we zoom out, a pattern becomes clear:

  • Wars made some societies crave order → German design
  • Wars made others crave warmth and joy → Italian design
  • Globalization made many societies crave efficiency → today’s industrial aesthetic
  • Digital overwhelm may make future generations crave emotion → future aesthetics we can’t yet predict

Design always carries the emotional DNA of its era.

And this is why I believe today’s flat, functional, industrial world isn’t inevitable.
It’s just what this moment in history is optimizing for.

The big events of tomorrow, whatever they may be, will give birth to new aesthetics, new approaches to beauty, and new philosophies of form. Some will be poetic. Some may be strict. Some might surprise us entirely.

But beauty will never disappear.
It only waits for its moment to return.

So where do we go from here?

As designers, we carry a quiet responsibility.

Not just to make things work,
but to make things worth having.
Not just to reduce friction,
but to increase meaning.
Not just to delight users temporarily,
but to elevate everyday life sustainably.

Beauty is not indulgence. Beauty is humanity.

And in a world rapidly becoming automated, standardized, and optimized, the most human thing we can do is bring back intentional beauty.

Maybe it starts with the next product we design.
Or the next interface.
Or the next street.
Or the next door, the next lamp, the next home.

Maybe it starts with simply noticing what we’ve already begun to forget.

I don’t know exactly what the world will look like 50 years from now.
But I do know this:

If we want a beautiful world, we have to design one: purposefully, courageously, and for everyone.
